## Challenged premise
**W7203485685.c005**: If an agent is robustly responsible for bringing about one outcome rather than another, its cognition must represent the first outcome as its own action.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:opus*

Ines, a reservoir controller, faces rising water during a storm. Opening the east spillway will flood farmland; doing nothing will let the dam overtop and flood the town. She studies hydrographs, weighs crop losses against lives, and concludes, in exactly these terms, 'The farmland should flood rather than the town.' She opens the spillway and the farmland floods. The inquiry finds her robustly responsible for the farmland flooding rather than the town: she foresaw it, compared it with the alternative, chose it knowingly, and could have done otherwise. Yet the outcome she represented, the farmland under water, was never represented as her action. It is a hydrological event downstream, and she represented it as one. The only thing she represented as her doing was turning the spillway wheel, and that is a different item from the outcome for which she is held responsible. Her responsibility for the flood rests on representing the flood as a foreseen consequence she was choosing between, not on representing the flood as her action.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: A concedes that the challenged premise is too strong: Ines need not represent the flood itself as her action. A replaces it with a requirement that she represent the selected outcome, under the relevant contrast, as the result of an act she performs and intends. — *This reply succeeds in accommodating the act-outcome distinction, but it abandons c005 rather than defending it. Its preservation of the divine argument depends on the further claim that representing divine actualisation as God's own act requires de se content.*
- **Defender B**: B argues that source attribution attaches to the act-outcome relation: Ines represents the flooding as issuing from her selected intervention, rather than representing the flooding itself as an action. B therefore proposes that responsibility requires representing the outcome as issuing from an act the agent performs or selects. — *The reply reaches a weaker premise consistent with the case, but it does not establish that the required agent-relative relation cannot be represented third-personally. That issue is central to the inference to irreducible de se cognition.*

## Open questions
- Is representation of an outcome as issuing from an agent's act necessary for robust contrastive responsibility, or can responsibility rest on knowledge of a foreseen consequence plus intentional action?
- Does representing an act-outcome relation as one's own require irreducible de se content, rather than a sufficiently rich third-personal representation?
- What distinguishes intending an outcome, intending an act while foreseeing an outcome, and selecting an outcome as part of contrastive deliberation for purposes of responsibility?
- Can the revised act-outcome requirement be applied to divine actualisation without assuming the de se conclusion it is meant to support?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the original premise conflates an agent's action with a distal outcome for which the agent is contrastively responsible, and that the defensible source-attribution condition concerns the agent's representation of an act-outcome relation. The case would use the reservoir example to motivate the revision, then anticipate the strongest defender's reply that representing an outcome as issuing from one's selected act still supplies the needed first-personal standpoint. It would need to show either that this relation can be represented without irreducible de se content, weakening the route to the divine conclusion, or that a principled account of agency makes such content necessary.
