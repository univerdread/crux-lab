## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:sonnet*

Consider Mara, a hospice chaplain whose late husband Tom was an atheist who never asked for her faith. Mara adopts a Vanstone-style picture: divine love is vulnerable, gives up control, and risks rejection, so God would not force relationship but would make the beloved's reciprocation possible without coercion. Schellenberg's analogy says a loving parent of an adult child who refuses contact would still pursue the child in a way that makes the relationship available, so God's silence is evidence against love. Mara grants the analogy for parents but notes that Tom's refusal to reciprocate did not make her love less vulnerable; her love was already a self-giving that accepts being unanswered. The analogy therefore imports a controlling, pursuit-based model of love that Vanstone's account explicitly rejects, so the analogy misdescribes the conception it claims to test, not because it is false of parental love but because it is not a faithful rendering of the vulnerable conception.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The parental analogy builds in a requirement that a loving agent make the beloved’s access to relationship available, whereas Mara’s case illustrates love that accepts being unanswered and does not treat reciprocation as something the lover must secure. The surviving criticism is that Schellenberg has not shown that this pursuit-to-access requirement is part of vulnerable divine love. — *Defender A conceded that this does not strictly show that the analogy misdescribes or is irrelevant to Vanstone’s conception. It establishes only an unproved transfer or burden-of-proof condition.*
- **Defender B**: A functional distinction can be drawn between availability and pursuit: availability gives the beloved an intelligible, non-coercive opportunity to recognize and answer a self-offer, while pursuit adds pressure or repeated intervention to overcome refusal. A vulnerable love may reject pursuit while still requiring availability. — *This reply reaches the central distinction but concedes that it does not establish that divine hiddenness supplies adequate non-coercive access. It therefore blocks the parental analogy only insofar as that analogy requires more than such access.*

## Open questions
- What makes an opportunity to recognize and answer divine self-offer sufficiently intelligible and non-coercive?
- Does Vanstone-style vulnerable love positively require divine availability, or merely permit it?
- Can Schellenberg’s parental analogy be reformulated to target non-coercive availability rather than pursuit or control?
- Under what conditions would divine silence be compatible with an available divine self-offer rather than merely tolerated by vulnerable love?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the decisive issue is not whether vulnerable love permits unanswered love, but whether it requires a recognizable and non-coercive form of divine availability. It would distinguish availability from pursuit, argue that Mara-style restraint undermines any inference from perfect love to pressure or repeated intervention, and assess whether Schellenberg’s analogy can instead support a thinner requirement of intelligible access. The strongest reply to anticipate is that kenotic love still seeks the beloved’s good and makes itself available; the paper would need to show either that this requirement is satisfied despite hiddenness or that the vulnerable conception does not entail it in the relevant sense.
