## Challenged premise
**W2072673546.c064**: Schellenberg’s analogical argument for P1 is unsuccessful because it distorts Vanstone’s vulnerable conception of divine love.

## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · anthropic:sonnet*

Consider Mara, a hospice chaplain whose late husband Tom was an atheist who never asked for her faith. Mara adopts a Vanstone-style picture: divine love is vulnerable, gives up control, and risks rejection, so God would not force relationship but would make the beloved's reciprocation possible without coercion. Schellenberg's analogy says a loving parent of an adult child who refuses contact would still pursue the child in a way that makes the relationship available, so God's silence is evidence against love. Mara grants the analogy for parents but notes that Tom's refusal to reciprocate did not make her love less vulnerable; her love was already a self-giving that accepts being unanswered. The analogy therefore imports a controlling, pursuit-based model of love that Vanstone's account explicitly rejects, so the analogy misdescribes the conception it claims to test, not because it is false of parental love but because it is not a faithful rendering of the vulnerable conception.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The parental analogy does not strictly falsify vulnerable love, but its premise that love must secure the beloved’s access to relationship is not shown to belong to that conception. Vulnerable love may permit nonbelief without abandoning the beloved. — *This retreats from the original claim that the analogy distorts Vanstone’s view. It establishes at most that the transfer requires independent support, not that pursuit or availability is incompatible with vulnerable love.*
- **Defender B**: Vulnerable love can distinguish non-coercive availability from pursuit: availability gives the beloved an intelligible opportunity to recognize and answer a self-offer, whereas pursuit adds pressure or intervention aimed at overcoming refusal. — *This distinction improves the objection by identifying a substantive residual condition, but it leaves open whether divine hiddenness supplies adequate recognizable access. It blocks the parental analogy as decisive without showing that hiddenness fits Vanstone’s conception.*

## Open questions
- What counts as an intelligible, non-coercive opportunity to recognize and answer a divine self-offer?
- Does Vanstone-style vulnerability entail an obligation to make relationship available, or only permit God to do so?
- Can a principled distinction between availability and pursuit be maintained in cases of nonresistant nonbelief?
- Does Schellenberg’s parental analogy establish a requirement beyond non-coercive availability, or merely assume one?

## Paper direction
A paper here would argue that the objection to Schellenberg’s parental analogy should be recast from a claim of distortion to a claim about an unestablished transfer: vulnerable divine love may require intelligible, non-coercive availability without requiring parental-style pursuit. The case would use Mara’s unanswered love to separate acceptance of nonreciprocation from efforts to secure response, while anticipating the strongest reply that a lover who is not recognizably available has not made relationship possible at all. It would need to show both that this availability–pursuit distinction is principled and that it yields a determinate standard by which divine hiddenness can be assessed.
