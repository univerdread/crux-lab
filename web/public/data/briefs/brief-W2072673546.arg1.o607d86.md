## Objection
*blind_thought_experimenter · openai:gpt-5.6-sol*

Elena’s adult son, Tomas, wrongly believes she abandoned him and refuses contact. Elena loves him deeply. She cannot force reconciliation without destroying its voluntary character, so she sends one clear message: she loves him, regrets their separation, and will answer whenever he calls. She keeps the same number, checks for messages, and bears the continuing risk that Tomas will reject her. Her vulnerability consists precisely in remaining genuinely available while leaving his response free. Now imagine a perfectly loving God relating to a resistant person in the same way. The analogy supports P1: perfect love requires an open path to relationship, even though it neither guarantees belief nor compels acceptance. Nothing about this comparison removes love’s exposure to rejection; divine openness is presented as the condition that makes such exposure possible. Thus the analogical route can preserve vulnerable love while supporting P1.

## Strongest responses and why they failed
- **Defender A**: The Elena case shows that openness, vulnerability, and non-compulsion can coexist, and openly offering oneself while surrendering control over acceptance is a genuine form of vulnerability closer to Vanstone’s kenosis than the initial reply allowed. But the analogy must still establish that such availability is obligatory rather than merely a permissible form of loving conduct. — *This reply concedes that the objection partly defeats the claim that Schellenberg’s analogy distorts vulnerable love. Its remaining force depends on overriding reasons in the divine case, which the analogy itself does not address.*
- **Defender B**: Elena’s continued availability shows that vulnerability does not require concealment and that love can permit the openness Schellenberg needs. Yet a particular loving mother’s conduct does not show that every perfectly loving agent must make conscious reciprocal relationship accessible to every capable person at every time. — *The distinction between permitted and required openness remains a serious limitation on the analogy, but it no longer supports the original distortion-based criticism. It instead requires an independent bridge to an obligation of accessibility.*
